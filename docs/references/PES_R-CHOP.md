## Header fields (fill-in-the-blank in original)
Care team contact, pharmacy contact, diagnosis, goal of treatment — blank fields for the
clinic to hand-write in.

## What R-CHOP is
Acronym: **R**ituximab, **C**yclophosphamide, doxorubicin (**H**ydroxydaunorubicin), vincristine
(**O**ncovin), **P**rednisone. Used for certain lymphomas (and other diagnoses per clinician).

| Drug | How it works | How given |
|---|---|---|
| Rituximab (Rituxan, Riabni, Ruxience, Truxima) | Helps immune system find/attack cancer cells via a surface protein target | IV infusion |
| Cyclophosphamide (Cytoxan) | Damages genetic material cancer cells need to multiply | IV infusion |
| Doxorubicin (Adriamycin, Hydroxydaunorubicin) | Damages genetic material cancer cells need to grow | IV infusion |
| Vincristine (Oncovin) | Prevents cancer cells from dividing/creating new cells | IV infusion |
| Prednisone | "Tells cancer cells to self-destruct" | Oral tablet(s) |

## Schedule
21-day cycle. R, C, H, O given Day 1. Prednisone daily Days 1–5 (with food, avoid evening
dosing — insomnia risk). Repeats Day 1 of next cycle.

## Common side effects (table: description + recommendations + when to call)
- Infusion reactions (boxed warning) — chills, itching/rash, breathing trouble, dizziness,
  fever ≥100.4°F, back/neck pain
- Low WBC / infection risk — hygiene, avoid crowds/sick contacts, possible growth-factor Rx;
  call for fever, chills, cough, sore throat, dysuria, unusual fatigue, skin infection
- Low platelets — gentle hygiene, electric razor, avoid NSAIDs, dental precautions; call for
  prolonged nosebleed, non-stopping cuts, gum bleeding, severe headache, blood in urine/stool/sputum
- Low RBC/Hgb — rest, avoid heavy machinery when tired; call for SOB, dizziness, palpitations,
  severe headache
- Fatigue — exercise, ask for help, relaxation techniques, sleep hygiene; call if it affects
  daily life / doesn't improve with rest / comes with dizziness+weakness
- Nausea/vomiting — small frequent bland meals, hydration ladder; call if vomiting >24h,
  dehydration signs, blood/coffee-ground emesis, severe abdominal pain
- Mouth sores — saltwater/baking-soda rinse, soft toothbrush, avoid irritants; call for pain/sores
- Diarrhea — track frequency, hydrate, low-fiber diet, avoid triggers; call for ≥4 BMs above
  normal in 24h, dizziness, bloody diarrhea
- Constipation — hydration, exercise, high-fiber diet; call after 3+ days or 48h post-laxative
- Nerve pain/tingling (peripheral neuropathy) — foot checks, well-fitting shoes, gentle
  aerobic activity; call for pins-and-needles/burning, weakness, balance trouble
- Hair loss — wigs/scarves/hats, sun protection; wig prescription available
- Bladder/kidney (cyclophosphamide) — hydrate 2L on Days 1–3; call for pink/red urine

## Select rare/boxed-warning side effects
Severe skin/mouth reactions, Hepatitis B reactivation, heart problems (cardiotoxicity —
doxorubicin has a lifetime max dose), PML, extravasation, tumor lysis syndrome — each with a
symptom list to escalate.

## Intimacy, fertility, pregnancy, breastfeeding
- Intimacy/body-image changes are normal; physical closeness remains safe
- Fertility may be affected — discuss preservation before starting
- Contraception: effective birth control during treatment + 12 months after last
  rituximab/cyclophosphamide dose (patient); partner contraception ≥6 months after last
  doxorubicin dose; pregnancy test before starting if applicable
- No breastfeeding during treatment + 6 months after last rituximab dose

## Handling body fluids/waste
Drug-specific precaution windows: 48h (cyclophosphamide), 7 days (doxorubicin), 3 weeks
(vincristine) post-dose — toilet hygiene, handwashing, separate laundry, pregnant-caregiver
avoidance.

## Additional info
Full medication disclosure to care team; doxorubicin causes transient orange/red urine;
secondary-cancer risk (e.g., leukemia); doxorubicin lifetime dose cap; sheet is not exhaustive
— FDA MedWatch reporting number given.

## Critique — what it does well

- **Comprehensive and clinically careful.** Every boxed warning and common toxicity is
  covered; "when to call" triggers are concrete (numeric thresholds like "fever ≥100.4°F,"
  "4+ BMs," "nosebleed >5 min") rather than vague — genuinely useful for triage.
  Plain-language drug-action one-liners ("tells cancer cells to self-destruct") are a nice
  simplification.
- **Actionable self-care steps** are specific and practical (baking-soda mouth rinse recipe,
  electric razor instead of blade, hydration targets).
- **Legally/clinically thorough on edge cases** most patients wouldn't think to ask about:
  fertility, contraception windows *per drug*, body-fluid handling windows *per drug*,
  lifetime dose caps, secondary-malignancy risk.

## Critique — where it falls short (the gap this project should close)

- **Organized around clinical taxonomy, not patient concerns.** It's structured
  drug-by-drug and side-effect-by-side-effect (a nurse's checklist), not around the
  questions a patient actually holds — "what will next Tuesday feel like," "am I dying or is
  this normal," "can I still work this week." A patient doesn't experience "low platelet
  count," they experience "I'm scared of this bruise."
- **No sense of time/trajectory.** Nothing tells the patient what a cycle *feels like* day
  by day (infusion day vs. day 3 crash vs. week 2 rebound) — side effects are listed as a
  flat catalog, not mapped onto the lived timeline. This is one of the biggest
  understandability gaps: patients want "what happens to me and when," not an
  alphabetized risk list.
- **Flat severity presentation.** Boxed warnings (life-threatening) sit in the same visual
  register as "hair loss" and "constipation" — a scared, newly-diagnosed reader can't
  easily triage what's common-and-manageable vs. rare-and-emergent at a glance.
- **No emotional acknowledgment.** Zero content validating fear, uncertainty, or the
  "isn't all-knowing at the end of the day" gap Dr. Reddy described — it's purely
  informational, no reassurance framing ("this is expected," "you're not alone in feeling
  this").
- **Static and generic.** One sheet, same for every patient, regardless of what they
  already know or what they specifically worry about — the opposite of the
  personalization (education level, topics of interest) this project is building.
- **Fill-in-the-blank fields assume a clinic conversation happens alongside it.** It's a
  companion handout, not a standalone explainer — it doesn't work if that 5-minute
  oncologist conversation is all a patient gets, which is exactly the gap Dr. Reddy
  described.
- **Text-only, no visual/audio channel.** No diagrams, no narration, nothing for
  low-literacy or visually-oriented patients — this is precisely the "weak UI" gap in the
  problem statement.
